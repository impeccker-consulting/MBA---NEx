The Foundation: Every Metric Starts With a Residual

Unlike our last session (which spanned two unrelated worlds), all four of today's metrics live in the same world — judging how well a regression model's predicted numbers match the actual numbers. They're four different lenses on the same underlying quantity: the residual.

🏠 Start Here: The MagicBricks Price Predictor

MagicBricks built a regression model to predict a flat's resale price from area, location score, age, and floor number. For every flat in the test set, we know the actual price and the model's predicted price. The gap between them is the residual: Residual = Actual − Predicted. A residual of +8 lakh means the model under-predicted by ₹8 lakh. A residual of −3 lakh means it over-predicted by ₹3 lakh.

⚠️ Why not just average the residuals? Because positive and negative errors cancel out! A model that's off by +20 lakh on one flat and −20 lakh on another has a beautiful "average error" of ₹0 — while being wildly wrong on every single prediction. This is exactly why MAE, RMSE, R² and Adjusted R² all exist: to measure error size without the errors cancelling each other.
Theory — The Big Picture

Four Metrics, Four Different Questions

Every one of today's metrics is computed from the same set of residuals — they just process them differently, which means each answers a slightly different business question.

MAE — "On average, how far off am I, in the original units?"Takes the absolute value of every residual and averages it. Treats a ₹10 lakh miss as exactly twice as bad as a ₹5 lakh miss — no more, no less.
RMSE — "How far off am I, punishing big misses extra hard?"Squares every residual before averaging, then square-roots back to original units. A ₹10 lakh miss counts 4× as much as a ₹5 lakh miss (because 10²=100 vs 5²=25) — outliers dominate.
R² — "What fraction of the variation in price did my model actually explain?"A unit-free score from (usually) 0 to 1, comparing your model against the laziest possible model: always predicting the average price.
Adjusted R² — "...and did I earn that R², or just buy it with extra variables?"Same idea as R², but penalises you for every additional predictor you throw in — because R² alone can never decrease no matter how useless a new variable is.

MAE & RMSE — Two Ways to Average the Damage

Both measure "typical error size" in the original units (₹ lakh, kg, days...) — but they disagree sharply on how much one giant mistake should count.

🎯

The Dartboard Analogy

Two players throw 10 darts each at a target. Player A lands consistently 5cm from the bullseye every time. Player B lands right on the bullseye 9 times — but once flings a dart 50cm off, hitting the wall. Both players have thrown roughly the same "average" miss if you're generous about it — but Player B has one throw that would embarrass anyone watching.

MAE treats every centimetre of miss equally — it would call these two players similar. RMSE squares the misses first, so Player B's one disastrous throw dominates the score — RMSE would flag Player B as clearly worse, and rightly so if that one wild throw matters (e.g. it broke a window).

The Formulas — Decoded

MAE = (1/n) Σ |Actual⁠ − Predicted|
Meaning
Average of the absolute residuals. Every error contributes proportionally to its size — a ₹10L error counts exactly 2× a ₹5L error, no more.
Robust to outliers
One freak bad prediction barely moves MAE, because it's only counted once, at its own size.
RMSE = √( (1/n) Σ (Actual − Predicted)² )
Meaning
Square every residual (this removes signs AND punishes big errors extra), average, then square-root to bring units back to normal (₹ lakh, not ₹ lakh²).
Sensitive to outliers
A single huge miss gets squared, dominating the sum. RMSE is always ≥ MAE — and the gap between them tells you how "spiky" your errors are.

R² — The Coefficient of Determination

MAE and RMSE tell you the error size in real units. R² asks a completely different question: "Compared to just guessing the average every time, how much better is my model?"

📐

The Lazy Benchmark Analogy

Imagine the laziest possible "model": ignore every predictor and just always guess the average price for every flat. This lazy model still gets some predictions roughly right by luck — flats really do cluster near the average. R² measures how much of the flat-to-flat price variation your real model explains beyond what this lazy average-guesser already captures for free.

R² = 0 means your fancy model is no better than the lazy average-guesser. R² = 1 means your model's predictions land exactly on every actual price — zero unexplained variation left.

The Formula — Decoded

R² = 1 − (SS_res / SS_tot)
SS_res
Sum of Squared Residuals — total squared error your model makes: Σ(Actual−Predicted)².
SS_tot
Total Sum of Squares — total squared error the lazy average-guesser makes: Σ(Actual−Mean)².
Reading it
If your model's errors (SS_res) are tiny compared to the lazy guesser's errors (SS_tot), the ratio is near 0, so R² is near 1 — your model explains almost all the variation.

Boardroom Challenge: Negative R² on New Data
Why R² can go below zero — and what it means
+15 pts▼

A demand-forecasting model shows R² = 0.71 on the training data used to build it — a solid result. Three months later, on fresh data from a new city launch, the same model scores R² = −0.18.

A negative R² sounds mathematically odd (since R² is "1 minus a ratio"). What does it actually mean here?
A
On this new data, the model's errors (SS_res) are actually LARGER than the errors from simply guessing the average every time — the model is doing worse than the laziest possible baseline, a red flag that it hasn't generalised to the new city
B
Negative R² is a calculation error and should never occur in practice
C
Negative R² simply means the model needs its threshold adjusted, similar to a classifier
Adjusted R² — R² That Can't Be Fooled

R² has a dangerous flaw: it can never go down when you add a new predictor — even a completely random, meaningless one. Adjusted R² fixes this by charging rent for every variable you add.

🎰

The "Kitchen Sink" Analogy

An overeager analyst adds every column available to a house-price model — including, say, "the last digit of the seller's phone number" and "number of pigeons seen outside on inspection day." These are obviously meaningless. Yet R² will still tick up, even if only by a tiny amount — pure random noise, given enough tries, will always appear to explain something in a finite sample, purely by chance.

Adjusted R² penalises the model for every variable added, based on how many degrees of freedom it costs. If a new variable doesn't pull its weight, Adjusted R² actually falls — even while plain R² keeps climbing.

The Formula — Decoded

Adjusted R² = 1 − [ (1−R²) × (n−1) / (n−k−1) ]
n
Number of observations in your sample (e.g. 30 flats).
k
Number of predictors currently in the model. As k rises (n fixed), the penalty term (n−1)/(n−k−1) grows — a bigger "tax" on Adjusted R².
The trade-off
Adding a genuinely useful predictor raises R² by more than enough to cover its tax — Adjusted R² rises too. Adding junk raises R² by only a sliver — not enough to cover the tax — so Adjusted R² falls.

Which model should HR actually prefer, and why?

Scenario 1 — Presenting to the Board

You need to tell the Board, in one sentence they'll immediately understand: "Our demand forecast is typically off by about ₹X lakh per store, per month." Which metric directly gives you that sentence?
A
MAE — it's literally "the average size of the miss," in the same currency units the Board already thinks in
B
R² — because 0 to 1 scores are always easier for boards to understand than currency figures
C
Adjusted R² — it accounts for the number of predictors, which is what boards care about most

Scenario 2 — A Rare but Catastrophic Failure Mode

A structural-engineering firm's model predicts load-bearing capacity. Nearly all predictions are excellent, but on rare unusual designs the model is occasionally wildly wrong — and those rare cases are exactly the ones with the highest safety risk. Which metric should weigh most heavily when selecting between two candidate models?
A
RMSE — its extra sensitivity to large errors makes it the right lens when rare big misses carry outsized real-world risk
B
MAE — since it's robust to outliers, it will correctly hide the effect of these rare failures
C
R² — because unit-free scores are always safer for engineering contexts

Scenario 3 — Two Models, Two Different Cities

You want to compare how well a "Sales ~ Ad Spend" model explains variation in Mumbai (where sales range ₹1–50 lakh) versus in a small town (where sales range ₹1–5 lakh). RMSE in Mumbai is much larger in absolute terms. Which metric lets you fairly compare "explanatory power" across these very different scales?
A
R² — being unit-free, it measures the proportion of variation explained regardless of whether sales are measured in lakhs or thousands, making cross-scale comparison fair
B
RMSE — larger absolute errors in Mumbai always mean a worse model there
C
MAE — comparing raw ₹ lakh figures across cities of different scale is always a fair comparison

Scenario 4 — The Analyst's Temptation

An analyst keeps adding more and more predictor variables to a regression, proudly reporting that R² keeps climbing with every addition. Your CFO, who has just sat through Sessions 2.1–2.7, asks the right follow-up question. What should it be?
A
"What happens to Adjusted R² as you add each variable — is it rising too, or is R² climbing only because you're adding noise?"
B
"What is the VIF of the outcome variable?" — VIF applies to the target variable, not the predictors
C
Nothing further to ask — a rising R² is always sufficient proof the model is improving
